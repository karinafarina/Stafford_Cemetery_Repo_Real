Established History

History of Stafford Springs Cemetery

Stafford Springs Cemetery reflects more than two centuries of remembrance, community stewardship, landscape design, and preservation. Originating as an early burial ground and substantially redesigned in 1883, the cemetery developed into a historic landscaped setting influenced by the nineteenth-century Rural Cemetery movement. Its winding drives, varied terrain, ponds, monuments, stonework, mature landscape, and historic architecture preserve an important part of Stafford's cultural history.

Early Origins (1811-1812)

The origins of Stafford Springs Cemetery trace back to 1811, when Miriam Alden selected her own burial site. Upon her passing in 1812, she became the first known burial on the property.

Lieutenant Elisha Alden subsequently set aside the surrounding land for use as a cemetery, establishing the foundation for a permanent place of remembrance.

Expansion and Community Stewardship

As the area developed, members of the community contributed to the expansion and preservation of the cemetery. The Converse family played a significant role by donating land and supporting the long-term care and stewardship of the grounds.

Incorporation (1882)

In 1882, the Stafford Springs Cemetery Association was formally incorporated, creating a structured framework for governance, expansion, and ongoing care. The incorporation marked a new phase in the cemetery's development, approximately seventy years after its first known burial.

Landscape Design (1883)

In 1883, Hartford landscape architect J. Alex McClunie redesigned the cemetery, introducing elements associated with the nineteenth-century Rural Cemetery movement. The redesign marked a transition from an earlier burial ground toward a more deliberately planned and picturesque cemetery landscape.

Winding drives, changes in elevation, naturalistic scenery, vegetation, monuments, views, and water features helped create a setting intended not only for burial, but also for remembrance, contemplation, and visitation. Many of these landscape characteristics remain visible in the cemetery today.

Civil War Memorial (1897)

The cemetery includes a Civil War memorial featuring a historic Rodman Gun, dedicated in 1897 to honor local contributions to the Union cause. The memorial also reflects the cemetery's long-standing role as a place of civic remembrance and community commemoration.

Early 20th Century Improvements (1912)

Stone entrance features were added in 1912, creating a more formal entrance to the landscaped grounds while reinforcing the cemetery's historic character and identity. These features remain an important part of the cemetery landscape.

Harwood Memorial Chapel (1929)

The Harwood Memorial Chapel, constructed in 1929, is a Gothic Revival stone structure gifted by Grace E. Harwood in memory of her parents, Charles F. and Ella Harwood. It remains one of the defining architectural features of the cemetery and an important component of the historic landscape.

Twentieth-Century Expansion (1964-1972)

The cemetery continued to evolve during the twentieth century as additional burial areas were developed to serve the community. Section 16 was extended in 1964, and New Section 1 was established in 1972.

These additions demonstrate the cemetery's continuing role as an active burial ground while preserving the character of the older historic landscape.

The Cemetery Today

Today, Stafford Springs Cemetery continues to serve lot owners and families while welcoming the public as a place for walking, quiet reflection, remembrance, and local history. The historic grounds preserve evidence of more than two centuries of community development and stewardship.

Recent preservation research has focused on documenting the cemetery's development, architecture, and landscape significance, including its relationship to the nineteenth-century Rural Cemetery movement. Through responsible stewardship, preservation, and long-term care, the Stafford Springs Cemetery Association is working to ensure that this historic landscape remains a lasting and respected part of the community.
